# tonic/gRPC vs QuicCrunch: measured head-to-head

**Verdict: stay on tonic.** On this hardware, with the same 1 KiB echo RPC, the
same 4-stream pipeline and the same client instrumentation, QuicCrunch wins
three of 36 cells, all at pipeline depth 1: one clear win at **1 connection,
depth 1, direct loopback** (30.9k vs 26.3k msg/s, +17 %, p99 184 µs vs 340 µs)
and two marginal +4–5 % results at 1 ms delay that are inside the host's
run-to-run noise. Every depth-64 and depth-1800 cell is won by tonic, by
1.5×–2.9×. At the two injected delays there is **no measurable throughput
difference at depth 1** (all ties within ±1 %), and QuicCrunch's p99 is never
better at depth ≥ 64. No crossover point exists in this matrix: increasing
pipeline depth and connection count makes tonic's advantage larger, not smaller.

That is the opposite of the depth-scaling hypothesis. The likely reason is in the
methodology section: four streams per connection plus 16 MiB HTTP/2 stream
windows leave little for head-of-line blocking to do, and the path is lossless,
so QUIC's loss recovery never runs.

Methodology, hardware, host load and every caveat are in
[`../comparison/README.md`](../comparison/README.md). Raw data:
`comparison/results/matrix.csv`, medians: `matrix-summary.md`, proxy
calibration: `matrix-proxy.csv`.

## Fairness contract

* One client binary, one instrumentation module, two backends. Enqueue, write
  hand-off and response timestamps are shared code.
* 1 KiB opaque payload echoed by both; 4 streams per connection; depths
  1/64/1800; connections 1/8/16/64.
* TLS on both sides with **server-certificate verification enabled**. No
  insecure shortcut on either side; the throwaway CA is generated by the bench
  script. The QUIC adapter now verifies server certificates by default
  (`RuntimeOptions::verify_server_cert = true`); the harness supplies the test
  CA explicitly and does not disable verification. See
  [`SECURITY.md`](SECURITY.md) for the opt-out rules and the remaining gaps.
* 3 s warm-up excluded; one shared 5 s measurement window; completed-only
  throughput; errors and timeouts excluded from percentiles; 3 repeats per
  cell, transports alternating order, medians reported.
* `RUST_LOG=warn`, effective log level recorded per run. No per-message logging
  exists at info or above in the measured path.
* 4 server workers both sides. Client CPU budget equalised at 10 threads
  (tonic: 10 tokio threads; QUIC: 8 runtime threads + 2 tokio threads).
* 1 KiB is the only payload size in the matrix; the proxy is calibrated with a
  0-byte baseline.

**Reading the deep cells.** At depth 1800, p50/p99 are queueing
(`depth × streams / throughput`), not transport RTT; the QUIC rows also carry a
large `cutoff` (requests still in flight at window close) because its lower
throughput cannot drain the pipeline in 5 s. Do not compare a depth-1800 p99
across transports as if it were per-request latency; compare throughput and
the same-depth tail shape.

## Hypothesis 1 — depth scaling without HTTP/2 head-of-line blocking

**No crossover.** QUIC/tonic throughput ratios at 4 workers:

| delay | depth 1 (1/8/16/64 conns) | depth 64 | depth 1800 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 ms | 1.17 / 0.83 / 0.88 / 0.78 | 0.45 / 0.58 / 0.59 / 0.49 | 0.43 / 0.46 / 0.50 / 0.34 |
| 1 ms | 1.01 / 1.04 / 0.94 / 1.05 | 0.83 / 0.62 / 0.62 / 0.64 | 0.66 / 0.62 / 0.51 / 0.41 |
| 10 ms | 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00 | 0.99 / 0.91 / 0.63 / 0.59 | 0.42 / 0.59 / 0.54 / 0.46 |

At depth 64 and 1800, 16–64 connections, tonic is **1.5×–2.9×** faster. The
ratios worsen for QUIC as depth and connection count rise. HTTP/2
head-of-line blocking does not materialise with 4 multiplexed streams per
connection and 16 MiB stream windows on a lossless loopback path; the dominant
term is how efficiently each stack drains a full pipeline, and tonic's h2/hyper
path does that better here.

## Hypothesis 2 — delay sensitivity and tails at 10 ms one-way

At 10 ms, **depth 1** (queue-free) is a dead heat:

| conns | tonic msg/s | QUIC msg/s | tonic p99 | QUIC p99 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 180 | 180 | 23,216 µs | 23,249 µs |
| 8 | 1,449 | 1,444 | 24,150 µs | 24,314 µs |
| 16 | 2,882 | 2,876 | 24,412 µs | 24,248 µs |
| 64 | 11,260 | 11,217 | 26,411 µs | 26,018 µs |

Differences are ≤1 %, i.e. **no measurable difference**. At larger depths QUIC's
p99 is worse (up to 3.2× at 16 conns/depth 64) because at equal depth its lower
throughput means more queueing ahead of each request, not because its ACK
handling is worse. On a lossless delay path there is no packet loss for QUIC to
recover from; this experiment cannot support a claim about QUIC loss recovery.

## Hypothesis 3 — connection-count scaling and SO_REUSEPORT fan-out

| connections | QUIC msg/s (depth 1) | tonic msg/s (depth 1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 30,883 | 26,312 |
| 8 | 45,447 | 54,693 |
| 16 | 57,171 | 64,898 |
| 64 | 57,472 | 74,065 |

QUIC scales 1.86× from 1 to 64 connections; tonic scales 2.81×. At 64
connections tonic is 29 % faster at depth 1 and 2.1× faster at depth 64.
SO_REUSEPORT fan-out does not beat tonic's accept distribution here. One caveat:
the QUIC client uses 8 runtime sockets, so 64 connections reach the server as 8
UDP source flows. That is how the adapter behaves, and it limits how much
fan-out the kernel can do.

Four workers help until ~16 connections; at 64 connections/depth 1800 the
single worker is already saturating the client and extra workers do not help.
The 1-worker 64-connection/depth-1800 cell was **flaky**: 1 run in the matrix
and 2 of 3 targeted reruns failed to establish all 256 streams (256 missing
streams, no data). Four workers passed every run. That reliability difference
matters more to a control plane than the throughput numbers.

## Proxy overhead

`tc netem` is unavailable (no `CAP_NET_ADMIN`), so one userspace proxy serves
both transports. The 0-byte baseline at 16 connections/depth 64 gives the
additive cost without queueing:

| path | tonic RTT p50 | QUIC RTT p50 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| direct, 0 ms | 798 µs | 1,266 µs |
| proxy, 0 ms | 799 µs | 1,274 µs |
| direct, 1 ms | 812 µs | 1,279 µs |
| proxy, 1 ms | 5,095 µs | 4,964 µs |
| direct, 10 ms | 806 µs | 1,285 µs |
| proxy, 10 ms | 22,610 µs | 22,790 µs |

The proxy's own cost at zero delay is **+0.5 µs (tonic) / +8 µs (QUIC)** and
negligible throughput loss for tonic; at 1 KiB and high rate it costs QUIC about
10 % throughput (113k → 101k msg/s), which is why the 0 ms cells are measured
directly on loopback. For delayed cells the configured 2 ms/20 ms RTT increase
arrives as 4.28 ms/21.8 ms (tonic) and 3.69 ms/21.5 ms (QUIC): a constant
**~1.5–2.3 ms residual per RTT** from tokio's ~1 ms timer granularity. It is
identical in kind for both transports and is disclosed, not silently subtracted.

## Host load

The run took ~45 minutes on a shared host. The 1-minute load average recorded
per cell ranged **3.09–14.39** (median around 8–10), driven by a co-tenant
`Suricata -T` run pinning one core, plus Home Assistant, Kodi and the agent
runtime. Every cell records its own load in the CSV, repeats alternate transport
order, and medians are used. Treat ±20 % as noise. The 2×–3× gaps that drive the
verdict are far outside that band; the ±1 % ties are not.

## What this does and does not say

* It does say QUIC has **no throughput advantage** for this RPC shape on
  loopback, and a **large disadvantage** once the pipeline is deeper than one
  request.
* It does say the only clear QUIC win is a low-concurrency, low-depth cell:
  +17 % throughput and −46 % p99 at 1 connection/depth 1.
* It does **not** measure loss recovery, connection migration, real WAN paths,
  mTLS/SPIFFE, CPU cost per byte, or control-plane work (fsync, Raft,
  serialisation). The echo RPC has no business logic on purpose.
* It does **not** find an HTTP/2 head-of-line-blocking penalty at 4 streams per
  connection. More concurrent streams per connection could change that, but that
  was not the requested matrix.

## Recommendation for distri-kv

**Keep the control plane on tonic.** The measured transport difference in the
control plane's regime is either a tie (10 ms, depth 1) or a 1.5×–2.9× tonic
throughput win (depth ≥64), and the only QUIC win is a single-connection case
that the control plane does not run in. distri-kv's control plane is fsync-bound:
a replicate/commit round trip is milliseconds of disk and consensus work, so the
tens-to-hundreds of microseconds that separate these transports are not the
bottleneck. Switching would buy no latency and would lose bulk throughput.

The non-performance blocker has changed: the adapter now supports client
certificate presentation, required server-side mTLS, SPIFFE-bound peer
identity reaching the method policy layer, fail-closed CRL revocation, and hot
rotation of server certificates, client certificates, trust anchors and CRLs
without dropping connections (see [`SECURITY.md`](SECURITY.md),
[`operations/certificate-rotation.md`](operations/certificate-rotation.md) and
[`BACKEND_MESSAGING.md`](BACKEND_MESSAGING.md)). OCSP is not implemented and is
documented as an explicit non-goal for this tQUIC/BoringSSL surface. The
flaky 1-worker/64-connection/depth-1800 cell remains an additional operational
risk, not a reason to migrate.

If QUIC is revisited, the experiments that could change this verdict are: many
streams per connection (>16) to actually stress h2 HOL blocking, a lossy WAN
emulation (1–5 % loss) to exercise QUIC recovery, and a real mTLS workload.
Those are not in this matrix and this document makes no claim about them.
